Make your own tank skin

A skin is a folder of PNG pictures: the body, the treads and the turret. This kit gives you blank templates with guides, an example skin you can import right now, and this short guide. Skins only change how tanks look on your own screen.

1. Try the example first

  1. Install TankTrouble Skin Tools (the main download on its GitHub releases page) and open tanktrouble.com.
  2. Click the small black Skins button, then Manage skins and Add a folder.
  3. Choose this kit's example-skin folder. The panel says Installed “Classic Tank”.
  4. Press Put it on my tank. Click the little picture to see it large. The classic tank is white, so it appears in your tank's colours, just like the game's own.

Open the files in example-skin next to the guides in templates/guides: every picture is drawn on the template's exact canvas, so you can see where art belongs.

2. How a tank is built

The game builds every tank from the same stack of pictures, bottom to top. You draw each picture facing up (the barrel points to the top of the picture); the game turns the whole tank.

  1. Body (base). One picture that never changes. Its centre, pixel (34, 42), is the middle of the tank.
  2. Treads (tread). One picture, placed twice: 26 px left and 26 px right of the centre, not mirrored. So do not paint light from one side that would look wrong on the other.
  3. Tread shade (treadShade0 to treadShade11, optional). Twelve see-through overlays drawn on top of each tread. This is the tread animation: while the tank drives, the game steps each tread's own frame number up (forward) or down (backward), wrapping 11 → 0; when the tank turns on the spot the two treads run in opposite directions. Draw a pattern that repeats along the tread and moves a little further in each frame, so frame 12 would look like frame 0. In the game's own frames the pattern repeats every 14 px and moves up by about 1.17 px per frame (14 ÷ 12) as the number rises; the classic tank does the same (a band every 14 px). Leave all twelve out to keep the game's own animation. Shade pictures are never tinted.
  4. Turret (turret0 to turret16). The game shows one turret picture at a time and swaps it to animate the weapon. A weapon animation is simply several turret pictures in a fixed order that you draw and the game plays (next section). The turret turns around the tank's centre, which is pixel (34, 57) of every turret picture (the turret canvas is 82 px tall and sits 16 px above the body's centre).

Muzzle: shots leave the barrel 50 px ahead of the tank's centre, which is pixel (34, 7) of a turret picture. Put the barrel tip within a few pixels of it. Hitbox: the game's tank box is 60 × 80 around the centre plus a 14 × 28 barrel box. The art never changes it, but keep your tank close to that size so you can still aim honestly.

3. The turret frames

Which turret picture shows when. The thumbnails are the example skin's.

{{table:turrets}}

How the animated ones play: turret1 then turret2 after each shot of the default gun (then back to turret0), the shotgun recoil goes 9, 10, 11, 10, 9 over about a second, and the gatling gun flips between 15 and 16, faster as it charges. You draw the stages; the game does the timing.

Colour: with "tint": "none" (the default for a skin without a skin.json) the weapons you did not draw show the game's own turret coloured with the player's turret colour, which can clash with your art. Drawing all 17 turret frames avoids that. The classic tank uses "player" instead: it is drawn in white and greys with a black outline, so every player's colour shows through, as in the game.

4. Draw and export

Settings that matter in every program: PNG, RGBA (with transparency), 8 bits per channel, sRGB, no interlacing, no cropping or trimming, no background layer, and the file named exactly like the picture (turret3.png, lower-case .png). Work at 1×, 2× or 4× the native size; Skin Tools reduces bigger art for you with clean edges, so 4× gives smoother edges.

The simple method that works everywhere: open templates/blank/turret_4x.png (the right size, transparent), put templates/guides/turret_4x.png on a layer above it, paint on a layer between them, hide the guides layer, and export. Repeat for each picture. To make 17 turrets, finish turret0, then edit copies of it for the other weapons.

Photoshop
  1. File ▸ Open the blank template, e.g. blank/turret_4x.png. Then File ▸ Place Embedded… and choose guides/turret_4x.png: it lands exactly on top. Lock that layer.
  2. Paint on a new layer below the guides (use a layer per part of the barrel if you like).
  3. Hide the guides layer. File ▸ Export ▸ Export As…: Format PNG, Transparency on, Metadata None, Convert to sRGB on, leave the canvas size alone. Name it turret3.png and so on.
  4. The layered route: open templates/psd/tanktrouble-skin-template-4x.psd, paint on the layer with the picture's name (turret layers are inside ART), save, then run the PSD helper (see Optional tools) to get all the PNGs and a skin.json in one go.
GIMP
  1. File ▸ Open the blank template, then File ▸ Open as Layers… and choose the matching file from guides/. Add a transparent layer for your art and keep the guides on top.
  2. Hide the guides layer. File ▸ Export As… (Shift+Ctrl+E), name it turret3.png.
  3. In the PNG options, leave Interlacing (Adam7) off, turn Save background color off, and choose pixel format 8 bpc RGBA.
Krita
  1. File ▸ Open the blank template. Bring the guides in as a layer above it: Layer ▸ Import/Export ▸ Import Layer… (or open the guides picture and copy-paste it). Paint on a new layer.
  2. Hide the guides layer. File ▸ Export… (Ctrl+Shift+E) as PNG, named turret3.png.
  3. In the PNG options keep Store alpha channel (transparency) on, Interlacing off, Save as indexed PNG off, and Force convert to sRGB on.
Procreate
  1. The easiest way is the PSD template: copy templates/psd/tanktrouble-skin-template-4x.psd to your iPad, open it in Procreate, and paint on the layers named after the pictures (leave the guides layers where they are). Export as PSD, bring the file back to a computer, and run the PSD helper (see Optional tools) to get the PNGs.
  2. Working from a PNG template also works: import guides/turret_4x.png as a canvas, add a layer above it, paint, hide the guides layer (and the background colour), and use Actions ▸ Share ▸ PNG. Procreate may not accept very small images; if the tread template is refused, use the PSD route.
  3. Do not export JPEG (it has no transparency).
Any other program (Aseprite, Paint.NET, Affinity …)

Open the blank PNG, paint inside it, and export a PNG with transparency, exactly the same size, with the right name. Check the result is not an indexed-colour PNG with a solid background.

Before you import: each file is exactly its canvas size · transparent background · facing up · 1 px clear border · named correctly · all 12 tread shades or none · every file at the same scale.

Semi-transparent edge pixels should carry your art's own colour, not white or black, or a thin halo shows on dark floors. If you see one, export again with no matte, or paint the edge on its own layer.

5. skin.json

A small text file saved next to your pictures. The example's, exactly as it is in example-skin/skin.json:

{{skinjson}}
KeyMeaning
schemaAlways 1. Required.
idRequired. Lower-case letters, digits and hyphens, 1 to 40 characters, not starting with a hyphen.
name, author, versionShown in the panel; up to 60 characters each. Optional.
tintWhat happens to the player's colour, per part (base, tread, turret). See below.
pixelScaleOptional check: 1, 2 or 4. It must match the size of your pictures, or the import is refused.
  • id is the skin's identity: installing a skin with an id you already have replaces it. Change it for every new skin.
  • tint says what happens to the player's colour, per part. "none": your picture is shown exactly as drawn. "player": your picture is multiplied by each player's colour, so paint it in white and light greys (white shows the full player colour, grey darkens it, black stays black; it can never brighten). The example uses "player", which is why it is plain white: that is how the game's own tank works, and a skin like this recolours with every player.
  • Optional "laser": { "colour": "#ff2f40", "glow": true } sets your laser's colour.
  • No skin.json? The folder name becomes the id and everything is shown as drawn.

Optional tools for artists

These need Node.js 20 or newer (a one-time download). They work offline, install nothing and change nothing outside the folder you name. Open a terminal inside this kit's artist-tools folder.

From a layered PSD to a skin

node psd-to-skin.mjs my-skin.psd --out my-skin --id my-skin --name "My Skin" --author "You"

Cuts every layer named after a picture (base, tread, treadShade0… turret16) out of the PSD template at the right place, exports the PNGs and writes skin.json. Add --scale 2 to export at half the PSD's detail. It tells you about anything it skipped or cut off.

From a texture atlas to a skin

If your pictures already sit in one sheet with a JSON list (the TexturePacker / Phaser / PixiJS format), convert them in one step. The kit includes a small atlas to try:

node atlas-to-skin.mjs example-atlas/classic-tank-atlas.json --out converted --id converted-example --preview

It reads the sheet and the JSON, puts every picture on its full canvas (trimmed pictures go back where they belong), brings everything to one scale (a smaller sheet, for example a half-size one, is refused unless you pass --upscale smooth or --upscale sharp: enlarging adds no detail, so it is always your choice), writes the PNGs and a skin.json, and then checks the result with the same rules Skin Tools uses on import. --preview also writes a contact sheet to look at. Nothing is assumed about the atlas: each picture is measured, bigger or 3× sheets are reduced exactly (a warning says whenever pictures were enlarged), and rotated pictures or sheets with a missing required picture are refused with a clear message. node atlas-to-skin.mjs --help lists the options (--tint, --scale, --upscale, --partial).

Beyond tanks

Skin Tools can also change shots, missiles, mines, explosions, shields and crates. Those pictures and their sizes are listed in the Skin Tools guide (docs/MAKING-SKINS.md in the repository, section 4) and work the same way: a PNG named after the picture, at its exact size, in your skin folder.
